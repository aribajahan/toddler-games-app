---
name: Pre-reader interactions
description: Interaction guidance for children who cannot yet rely on written button labels.
---

Make the visible object itself the control whenever a child is choosing between objects or groups. For binary abstract choices, use familiar symbols such as a check and an X rather than text labels or directional words.

**Why:** The intended audience includes children who are still learning to read. The user explicitly approved direct object tapping for Math Mix instead of “Left group” and “Right group” buttons.

**How to apply:** Use this pattern across child-facing games when the action can be represented visually. Keep descriptive accessibility labels for assistive technology even when visible labels are removed.

For early-literacy activities, keep the visible instruction aligned with the spoken instruction. Present one clear classification at a time, provide replayable audio, and preserve already-correct progress after a mistake.

**Why:** Mismatched spoken and written directions create uncertainty for children and adults helping them. Multi-step sorting adds avoidable interaction overhead, and erasing correct letters makes practice feel punitive.

**How to apply:** Write the same complete action, target, and choices that the audio speaks, or a very close equivalent. Advance sound-sorting pictures one at a time and let the child retry only the incorrect choice.

For “make a number” activities, show a visible bank of objects and a clear destination, then let the child move the requested quantity into it.

**Why:** Abstract empty slots made “Make 7” feel vague. Moving visible objects from a bank into a basket makes the action and progress concrete.

**How to apply:** Keep every source object directly draggable, show accepted objects accumulating in the destination, remove them from the bank, and return off-target drops gently.
